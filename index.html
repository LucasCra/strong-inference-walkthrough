Research method, drawn as a working cycle

Strong inference, one step at a time

Strong inference is a way to do research: write several rival explanations, design an experiment that rules some of them out, then repeat with what is left. Press Play to watch an invented ML project move through the workflow, and see what each step needs, does, produces and stores.

An untested concept. Nobody has run this workflow on a real problem. Every run and number in the walkthrough is invented, except the two facts marked (real). The “needs”, “outputs” and “stores” fields, and some “why here” text, are this page’s summary of the diagram and its explanation.

How knowledge is kept, and what limits it

Each kind of record has a place where it is written and a place where it is read. All but the rescue log can also be undone.

The life of a finding

  1. SeenStep 8 · openThe standing checks show it. It is written with its size and run.
  2. KeptStep 9 · openIt is recorded, whatever the gates decide about the hypotheses.
  3. ReopenedAny time · openChanged conditions turn an old exclusion into a new finding.
  4. DecidedStep 3 · 3 waysRepeats and needed: promote. Repeats, not needed: defer. Does not repeat: drop.
  5. TestedSteps 4 to 7 · promotedIt becomes a how or why question with rivals and a new experiment.
  6. UsedStep 10 · closedThe surviving explanation changes something, and the change is measured.
  7. ReportedStop · unchangedEvery finding is listed with its status, so stopping cannot lose one.

Three obligations hold this together: step 9 needs a findings entry, even if empty; step 3 needs a decision on each open finding; Stop needs every finding’s status. These are rules of the method. For now they rest on the researcher’s discipline, and nothing checks them.

Where each piece comes from

SourceWhat it adds
Platt, 1964Alternative hypotheses, the crucial experiment, the clean result, recycling, and the logical tree in a daily notebook.
Chamberlin, 1890Hold every rational explanation at once, and allow several causes in varying proportions.
Fudge, 2014The front end: observe, question, predict, and the gates on each.
Popper, 1963Start from a problem, forbid outcomes, risk the prediction, never rescue ad hoc. Marked with an orange dot on the map.
Railsback“None of the above” as a placeholder, and untested is not excluded.
Lakatos, Mayo, MeehlRepeated rescue degenerates; severity; a direction alone risks little. Their notes rest on summaries, one posted section and memory, not on a primary text.

Left out on purpose

These wait until a first working skeleton shows a need.

  • Independent checking: who runs, who judges, who sees what
  • Reusing data across cycles, which can overfit even a holdout set
  • Making sure rival hypotheses really differ
  • Choosing what is worth doing next, by value and cost
  • Whether the measure captures the objective
  • Whether all these rules pay for their cost
  • Thresholds: how many hypotheses, seeds or checks are enough
  • Recording pruned hypotheses
  • Rivals that predict the same thing, which only step 6’s outcome table can catch
  • Three reasons to stop sharing one gate: success, an empty budget and a wrong objective
  • Outside criticism and independent replication
